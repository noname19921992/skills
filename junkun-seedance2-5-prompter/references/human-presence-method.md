# Authentic Human-Presence Performance Method

## Definition

"Authentic human presence" does not come from skin pores, handheld shake, random blinking, or a large number of micro-expressions. It comes from a credible causal chain: a character receives information, understands what it means, briefly leaks a genuine bodily reaction, then chooses how to act. The effect of one moment carries into the next.

The goal is not to keep the character moving. The goal is to make every change motivated, with both control and failures of control visible.

## 1. Begin with Objective and Defense

First define the character's actionable objective in the moment, for example:

- Keep the conversation ordinary so the other person does not ask more questions.
- Obtain an admission without appearing to still need it.
- Finish the task at hand as a way to avoid looking at someone.
- Leave the scene while waiting for one sentence that would make them stay.

Then define how the character protects themself. They might keep folding clothes, wipe a cup, organize a backpack, maintain a polite speaking pace, stay by the doorway, watch the workbench, or handle an object. Protective behavior creates a baseline for micro-reactions. Without a baseline, pauses and cracks are difficult to perceive.

## 2. Use a Human Reaction Chain

Key reactions follow:

`perceive stimulus → comprehension pause → involuntary leak → deliberate response → self-repair → emotional residue`

- **Perceive stimulus**: Hear a keyword, see an object move, feel contact, or notice a spatial change.
- **Comprehension pause**: From a fraction of a beat onward, depending on the relationship, degree of surprise, and how prepared the character is.
- **Involuntary leak**: Breath catches, gaze is pulled toward a meaningful object, a hand action stops late, grip pressure changes, or the voice starts unsteadily.
- **Deliberate response**: Deny, change the subject, resume the task, move closer, step back, raise the voice, or become deliberately calm.
- **Self-repair**: After recognizing the leak or loss of control, lower the voice, withdraw the hand, pick up the object again, or avoid eye contact.
- **Emotional residue**: Breathing, posture, action speed, and attention in the next beat remain affected by what just happened.

Do not mechanically force all six stages into every beat. Simple reactions may combine stages, but the stimulus must precede the result and the ending state must carry into the next beat.

## 3. Slight Desynchronization Feels More Natural

Channels of human reaction often have slight offsets:

- The eyes are drawn to a keyword first; the hand stops half a beat later.
- The inhale breaks first; the voice tightens afterward.
- Only after finishing a line does the character realize it was too forceful; the gesture hangs, then retracts.
- The ordinary task resumes, but faster or with more force than before.

Do not assign a blink, nod, or eyebrow change to every word. Do not fix all reaction delays to the same number of seconds; timing should serve the meaning of the moment.

## 4. Limit the Number of Micro-Actions

Select 1–3 distinctive signals per performance beat. The usual priority is:

1. Primary action or interruption of action;
2. Change in weight, distance, or posture;
3. Breathing, voice, and speaking rhythm;
4. Gaze and hands;
5. Local facial change only when needed.

Avoid instructing raised brows, furrowed brows, blinking, nostrils, mouth corners, swallowing, fingers, and shoulders at the same time. Anatomical checklists tend to produce puppet-like performance. FACS or Action Units may be used only as a close-up calibration layer. Begin with motivation, trigger, and visible action in natural language, then use a small number of AUs to disambiguate; never treat an AU list as the performance itself.

## 5. Preserve Asymmetry and Incompletion

Useful behaviors include:

- One shoulder relaxes before the other.
- The lips part to answer, but no sound follows.
- A reaching hand changes its decision halfway.
- A smile appears and does not vanish immediately, but gradually loses support.
- An already organized object is arranged one more time.

These changes must arise from character intent or pressure. Do not add random twitching, frequent gaze drift, deliberate defocus, unmotivated stumbling, or constant camera shake for the sake of "realism."

## 6. Give Actions Inertia and Memory

The character existed before the shot began and remains the same body after a cut:

- A held object stays the same, and positional changes have a visible process.
- An interrupted action either resumes, fails, or is replaced by another decision.
- An outburst is followed by awareness of lost control and an attempt to contain it.
- Crying, laughter, anger, and fatigue do not return instantly to zero in the next shot.
- Clothing, hair, mud, water, sweat, injuries, and spatial distance accumulate with action.

Build a state chain for a key prop, for example: `sofa arm → accidentally knocked off by the palm → floor → remains there afterward`. The same prop must not fall twice or return to its original position without cause.

## 7. Multi-Character Scenes Are Mutual Regulation

Characters continually read one another:

- One person's answer comes too quickly, making the other quieter and more certain.
- One person avoids looking at an object, allowing the other to realize they remember.
- One person's sudden outburst makes the other stop arguing or change distance.
- New information may remove the support beneath an aggressive posture without erasing the preceding anger.

Give listeners sufficient screen time. Visible characters move their lips only while speaking their own lines. When listening to off-screen dialogue, keep the mouth closed and respond through gaze, breath, hand action, and posture. Multiple characters should not produce the same standardized reaction at the same intensity in the same instant.

## 8. Dialogue Must Include Performance Time

The duration budget includes preparation before speaking, speech, pauses, interruption, hearing, understanding, and aftermath. Text fitting when read aloud does not mean the character can perform it naturally.

Dialogue may include conversational fragments, self-correction, repetition of ordinary words, and unfinished sentences. In emotional scenes, the decisive moment often occurs after the line: the character resumes the same task but cannot recover the previous rhythm.

For Mandarin emotional dialogue, roughly 3–5 Chinese characters per second can serve as a coarse overload check. Arguments may run faster, while hesitation, crying, subtext, and action require slower delivery. Use this figure only to identify obvious overload; it does not replace a read-through and contextual judgment.

## 9. Sound and Silence Are Also Performance

Prioritize natural breathing, swallowing, clothing friction, contact between hands and objects, room tone, and near/far spatial relationships. Dialogue need not sound studio-smooth; the onset of the voice, insufficient breath support, or a softened ending can carry character state.

Music must not announce emotion before the character does. Key comprehension, containment after losing control, and unresolved endings often require ambience or a brief silence. If music is used, specify its entrance, instrumentation, dynamics, and relative volume.

## 10. Treat the Camera as an Observer

Ensure the audience can read causality before pursuing elaborate camera movement:

- Establish the characters, props, distance, and spatial direction.
- Leave reaction time after key dialogue; do not cut away too early.
- Move closer gradually as emotional pressure increases, but do not rush toward the face without motivation.
- Trigger a cut through a change in speaking control, comprehension, concealment, object action, or relationship.
- Within a single shot, change framing only through plausible camera or character movement.
- In multi-character dialogue, maintain the axis, eye-line height, and left-right direction.

Locked-off cameras, stable pushes, and handheld work can all convey human presence. Do not equate documentary feeling automatically with shake, missed focus, or rough exposure.

## 11. A Compressed Example

Abstract version:

> After hearing the truth, she is shocked and devastated, and tears run down her face.

Executable version:

> After hearing, "I knew all along," she does not answer immediately. The inhale she was preparing stops in her chest; her gaze is drawn first to the keys in the other person's hand, while the hand pulling her backpack zipper stops half a beat later. She draws the zipper slightly upward again as if to continue leaving, but does not turn. When she starts her next line, her volume is lower than before, while the anger from the previous moment remains in her jaw and stance.

The second version does not force tears or overload facial anatomy, yet it specifies the stimulus, comprehension, leak, repair, residue, and character objective.
